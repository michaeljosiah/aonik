Guardian relationships and verifiable parental consent

Arke Kids processes children's data and generates content for them, so someone with legal authority over that child has to agree to it — verifiably, which is a term of art and not a checkbox. This spec adds the relationship that carries authority, the consent record that can actually evidence it, and the date on which both end because the child has grown up. It is a precondition of launching Arke Kids at all, not a feature of it.

1. Summary

Spec 086 gives Arke Kids a family: a Group whose kind is family, whose members are parties, and where a child is a member with a PartyId and no UserId — a person in the system who cannot authenticate. That models the household. It does not model authority: nothing in the platform says which adult may act for that child, or records that they agreed to anything.

This spec adds three things and one deadline:

  • A Guardian relationship carrying legal authority over a child party — distinct from the kinship codes, because parental authority is not the same thing as parenthood.
  • Verifiable consent: a record that can evidence who consented, how they were verified, to what purpose, and under which version of the terms.
  • Purpose-scoped, revocable consent rather than one blanket agreement at sign-up.
  • An age-up transition, because guardian authority is not permanent and a product that never ends it is quietly wrong about whose data it holds.
Why this blocks a launch rather than following one Consent obtained the wrong way is not consent, and it cannot be repaired retroactively. If Arke Kids ships with a checkbox and later needs real verifiable consent, every existing account must be re-consented — a campaign with a predictable outcome, since a meaningful share of parents will simply not respond, and their children's accounts must then stop processing. It is far cheaper to be right at the start than to run that campaign later.

2. The legal frame, and the limits of this document

Read this before treating anything below as settled This is an engineering specification written by an engineer, not legal advice. It sets out what the relevant regimes appear to require and designs a system capable of satisfying them, so that counsel has something concrete to correct rather than a blank page. Every threshold, method and retention period here must be confirmed by a qualified data-protection lawyer before Arke Kids processes a single child's data, and the open decisions in §17 are weighted deliberately toward the questions only counsel can close.

Three regimes plausibly apply, and they do not agree with each other:

RegimeWhereWhat it turns on
UK GDPR + the Age Appropriate Design Code UK Consent age 13. The Children's Code adds fifteen standards covering default settings, data minimisation, profiling, nudge techniques and transparency — and it applies to services likely to be accessed by children, not only those aimed at them.
EU GDPR Article 8 EEA Consent age 16 by default, which member states may lower to no less than 13 — and several have. The threshold therefore varies within the EEA.
COPPA US Under 13. Requires verifiable parental consent before collection, and enumerates the acceptable methods — §8. This is the strictest on method.

The design consequence is that the threshold is configuration, the mechanism is code. Build one consent machine capable of the strictest method, and let jurisdiction decide which age it triggers at and which method is sufficient. Building to the loosest and adding strictness later means rebuilding.

4. Scope (in / out)

4.1 In scope

  • The Guardian relationship type, its authority semantics, and its lifecycle.
  • Age representation on a party, and the assurance signal behind it.
  • A consent record capable of evidencing verifiable parental consent, replacing the current PartyConsent shape.
  • Purpose scoping, terms versioning, revocation and re-consent on material change.
  • The verification methods themselves, and their evidence records.
  • The age-up transition and what it does to authority, data and ownership.
  • Enforcement: what the platform refuses when consent is absent, expired or withdrawn.

4.2 Out of scope

  • Content safety. What a model may generate for a child is Spec 096. Consent governs whether we may process; safety governs what we may produce. Both are required and neither substitutes for the other.
  • Identity proofing of adults generally. This spec verifies a guardian for the purpose of consent; it is not a KYC programme.
  • Age assurance as a product surface. Estimating age from behaviour or imagery is a large, separately contentious field. §6 takes the declared-plus-corroborated route deliberately.
  • Any Arke Kids UI. The platform provides the mechanism and the refusals; the product presents them.
  • Retrospective consent for existing accounts. There are none. That is the point of doing this first.

5. Three ages, one product

A single child in a single family may sit on either side of the line depending on where they live. The platform must therefore treat the threshold as data: 

ConsentAgeThreshold  per jurisdiction, resolved from the SUBSCRIBER's
                     jurisdiction, not the device's locale
    GB  -> 13
    IE  -> 16          (member-state choice)
    ES  -> 14          (member-state choice)
    US  -> 13          (COPPA, with the strictest METHOD requirements)
    ...  -> 16         (conservative default for anything unmapped)
The default must be the strict one An unmapped or unknown jurisdiction resolves to 16 and the strictest verification method. This is the opposite of how defaults are usually chosen and it is deliberate: a wrong-way default here means processing a child's data without valid consent in a jurisdiction nobody checked, and discovering it through a regulator. Being over-strict costs friction for some families; being under-strict is a breach.
  • Jurisdiction comes from the subscribing guardian's billing or residence data, which we already hold, and never from an IP address or a browser locale — both of which are trivially wrong and trivially spoofed.
  • A change of jurisdiction re-evaluates consent: a family moving from the UK to Ireland may find a 14-year-old who was self-consenting now requires a guardian.
  • The threshold is tenant-overridable upward, never downward. An operator may be stricter than the law; the platform will not let them be laxer.

6. Knowing the age is itself a risk

The paradox at the centre of this spec Every obligation here turns on whether a user is a child — so the system must know. But establishing that someone is a child means collecting and retaining data about a child, which is the exact category of processing the regime exists to restrict. Age verification schemes that demand government ID from families are a well-documented way to turn a privacy obligation into a privacy incident.

The route taken, and why it is the proportionate one:

OptionAssessment
Full age verification — documentary proof for every child rejected Disproportionate for a storytelling product, creates a database of children's identity documents, and deters exactly the careful parents we most want.
Self-declared age, unverified rejected Provides no assurance at all, and is the arrangement regulators have repeatedly found inadequate.
Declared age + verified guardian chosen The child's age is declared by a verified adult who has asserted legal authority over them. The assurance attaches to the adult — who can be verified proportionately — rather than to the child, whose data we then minimise.
  • Store the minimum that answers the question. Where a birth year suffices to place a child in an age band, store the year and not the full date. A product that needs to say "happy birthday" can ask separately, with its own consent.
  • Two separate bandings, deliberately.
    P0 — one field cannot carry both partitions, and a previous draft made it try Party.AgeBand was defined here as the three-way consent partition — under-13, 13-to-threshold, at-or-over — with a note that Spec 096 consumes it. But 096 needs four safety partitions: under 6, 6–9, 10–12, and 13-to-majority. A six-year-old and a twelve-year-old share a consent band while needing completely different prompts and thresholds, and the safety banding deliberately does not change at the consent threshold. The two documents were prescribing incompatible partitions for a single stored field.
    FieldPartitionAnswersOwner
    ConsentBandunder-13 · 13-to-threshold · at-or-overWho may consent, and to whatThis spec
    SafetyBandunder-6 · 6–9 · 10–12 · 13-to-majorityWhat may be generated, and how strictlySpec 096

    Both derive from the same attested date and both are stored with their own transition dates. They are not derivable from one another and no code should try: they answer unrelated questions, change on different dates, and are set by different authorities — a jurisdiction's legislature for one, our own product judgement for the other.

  • A child's declared age is attested by the guardian and the attestation is part of the consent record, so a false declaration is a traceable act by an identified adult rather than an anonymous checkbox.

7. The guardian relationship

A new Guardian code in PartyRelationshipTypes, from the guardian party to the child party. It is not a synonym for the kinship codes and the distinction is load-bearing:

Parental authority is not parenthood Mother and Father describe biology or family structure. Guardian describes legal authority to act for a child. They diverge constantly and in both directions: foster carers, kinship carers, adoptive parents pre-order, step-parents with a parental responsibility agreement, grandparents raising grandchildren, and — the direction people forget — a biological parent who does not hold parental responsibility. A system that infers authority from Mother gets real families wrong, and gets them wrong in the direction of granting access to someone who should not have it.
PropertyRule
DirectionFromPartyId = guardian, ToPartyId = child. Never inferred in reverse; a Child kinship edge does not imply guardianship.
MultiplicityA child may have several guardians. Families are not uniform and a design admitting only one is wrong about most of them.
AuthorityEach active guardian may act for the child independently. See below on disagreement.
EstablishmentCreated only through IConsentService's enrolment path, by an already-verified guardian or an operator with an audit record — never through the generic relationship API. See §7.2, which is the most dangerous thing in this spec.
EndIsActive = false on revocation, on operator action, or at the age of majority — not at the consent threshold, which is a different and earlier date. §11.1.

7.2 Guardian must not be an ordinary relationship code

P0 — adding it to the shared set creates a privilege-escalation path

PartyRelationshipTypes.Codes is validated by the generic PartyService.CreateRelationshipAsync, which checks membership of the set and nothing else — no verification, no authority check. That is correct for the codes it was built for, which are descriptive: asserting someone is a Sibling grants no access.

But that path is fed caller-supplied relationship codes by ordinary finance workflows — OrderService:765-773 passes request.RelationshipTypeCode straight through, and PayoutBeneficiaryService:646-648 does the same. So the moment Guardian joins the set, an ordinary order-creation request can mint a guardian edge that IGuardianshipReader then trusts for access to a child's data.

The bug is the assumption that all relationship codes are alike. Every existing one describes; this one authorises, and a value that grants authority cannot travel through a path built for values that do not.

Three rules, and the first is the one that matters:

  • PartyService refuses Guardian outright. The generic create and update paths reject it with "guardian relationships are created through the consent service" — so the finance workflows are safe by construction rather than by their callers behaving. Failing closed in the shared path is the control; anything else relies on every future caller knowing.
  • Only IConsentService may write one — through exactly two operations, both of which verify first.
    P0 — enrolment alone made multiplicity unreachable A previous draft allowed a Guardian edge to be created only inside EnrolChildAsync, which creates a new child. So once a child existed there was no authorised operation that could add a second guardian — you would have had to enrol a duplicate child. That makes §7's multiplicity requirement and the entire withdrawal-wins rule in §7.1 unimplementable, since both presuppose a child can have more than one guardian. The rule intended to stop unverified edges also stopped the legitimate ones.
    • EnrolChildAsync — creates the child and their first guardian (§12.2).
    • AddGuardianAsync(childPartyId, newGuardianPartyId, verification) — targets an existing child. The new guardian is verified to the same standard as the first (§8); an existing active guardian must authorise the addition, so authority cannot be granted to oneself; and the operation is audited with both parties recorded.
    • The generic-path refusal is unchanged: PartyService still rejects Guardian outright, so neither operation weakens §7.2.
  • Privileged codes are marked as such in the type definition, not remembered. A future code carrying authority inherits the refusal automatically instead of re-learning this lesson — and a test asserts that every privileged code is rejected by the generic path.
The general lesson, which this platform has now learned twice Spec 089 §8.1 found the same shape: read/write access had been left as a product-interpreted term while the endpoint was the platform's, so the check was decoration. Here the value is privileged while the path is generic. Both are cases of an authority decision sitting somewhere that was not built to make one.

7.1 When guardians disagree

Two guardians, one revokes consent and the other does not. This will happen — separated parents are common and the product is exactly the kind of thing they disagree about.

The rule: withdrawal wins Any single active guardian may withdraw consent, and withdrawal takes immediate effect regardless of the others. Re-granting requires a fresh consent, and does not silently undo the withdrawal — the product surfaces that a guardian previously withdrew, so re-granting is a deliberate act rather than a race.

This is asymmetric on purpose. Processing a child's data over an objection from someone with legal authority is the worse error, and it is the one that ends up in front of a regulator. The platform is not equipped to adjudicate family disputes and must not try; it takes the conservative branch and leaves the dispute to the people who can actually resolve it.

8. What "verifiable" means

This is the single most likely thing to get wrong "Verifiable parental consent" is a term of art with enumerated methods, and a tickbox saying "I am this child's parent" is not one of them. Nor is an email address alone, in the general case. A system that records agreement without evidencing who agreed has collected a click, not a consent — and the record it leaves is worse than useless, because it looks like compliance in an audit until someone reads the schema.

The methods the platform supports, ordered by strength:

MethodHow it evidencesUse
Payment instrument A verified card or mandate held by the consenting adult, exercised through a real transaction or authorisation — not merely captured. primary The subscribing parent already has one (Spec 087), so the strongest available method is a by-product of paying. This is the happy path and it should be the default.
Government ID check Document verification through the existing screening path, with the document deleted after the check and only the outcome retained. fallback For guardians on a £0 tier with no payment instrument.
Signed consent form A returned form, electronically or physically signed, matched to a named adult. fallback Slow, high-friction, and genuinely useful for edge cases such as institutional carers.
Verified video or voice call Live contact with trained personnel, with an outcome record. deferred Requires staffing that does not exist. Recorded for completeness.
Email-plus Confirmation by email, plus a second step after a delay — a follow-up confirmation to the same address, or a second channel. restricted Permitted only where the child's data is never disclosed to a third party. §9 explains why that condition almost never holds here.

9. Why generative AI raises the bar

The finding that determines which methods are actually available

The lighter email-plus method exists for services that keep children's data internal. Arke Kids does not: a child types a story idea, and that text is sent to a third-party model provider — an LLM vendor for the story, an image or video provider for the pictures, a voice vendor for narration. Under any ordinary reading, sending a child's own words to another company is disclosure to a third party.

So the sliding scale does not slide. Arke Kids sits at the higher bar by construction, and the payment-instrument or ID methods are the real options. Anyone planning around email-plus should stop now rather than after building it.

Three consequences follow, and all three are cheaper to accept than to discover:

  • The consent text must name the disclosure. Not "we may share data with service providers", but that what the child writes is sent to named external companies to generate the result. Consent to an unstated disclosure is not consent to it.
  • Changing providers can be a material change requiring re-consent — which makes the provider list part of the consent record's versioned terms, not an implementation detail (§10).
  • It is an argument for local generation. Arke Studio 020 already runs models locally. Content generated on the family's own machine is not disclosed to anyone — which is a materially better privacy posture for a child-facing product and deserves weight in the model-C decision, not only a cost comparison.

10.1 One purpose, one row

Blanket consent is the failure mode this shape exists to prevent. A guardian who agreed to their child generating stories has not thereby agreed to marketing, to their child's drawings being used to improve a model, or to sharing with grandparents. Purposes are separate rows, independently revocable:

PurposeCoversRefusable independently?
service-coreOperating the account at all — storing the child's profile and their work.No. Withdrawing it closes the account, and the product says so plainly.
generation-disclosureSending the child's inputs to named third-party providers (§9).Yes — and refusing it leaves a usable product only if local generation exists. Another argument for it.
sharing-externalSharing a child's work outside the family, e.g. the grandparent flow in the capability brief.Yes. Off by default, per the Children's Code's default-settings standard.
safety-classification Rev 3Sending a child's generated content to an external classifier so it can be judged safe before delivery (§12.3).No while on-device classification does not exist — refusing it means refusing generation, because unclassified content is never delivered to a child.

marketingAny promotional contact.Yes. Off by default. Never to the child.
The default posture is off Everything except service-core defaults to not granted. This is the Children's Code's "high privacy by default" standard, and it is also simply the right answer: a parent who has not been asked has not agreed, and pre-ticked boxes are the canonical example of consent that is not freely given. improvement deserves particular care — training on children's creative work is the kind of thing that reads badly in a headline even when it is lawful, and the honest default is not to.

10.2 Consent goes stale

  • A material change invalidates it — at publication, not on reply. New purposes, new categories of data, or a new third-party disclosure require fresh consent under the new TermsVersion. Publishing that version revokes the affected active grants immediately; it does not wait for a replacement, because waiting means continuing to process under withdrawn terms for everyone who does not reply.
    The operational consequence, which is the point rather than a side effect Publishing a material terms change stops processing for every affected family until they re-consent. That is not something to engineer around — it is what a material change means, and making it cheap would make consent meaningless. Practically: publication needs advance notice and a comms plan, and the bar for calling a change "material" is a decision with real weight rather than a formality.
  • Periodic re-confirmation for long-lived accounts, so a parent who set something up years ago is asked again. Proposed: annually.
  • Verification staleness is separate from consent staleness. A payment instrument that has expired does not retroactively invalidate the consent it verified, but it cannot verify a new one.
  • Withdrawal is immediate and honoured everywhere, including in-flight generation: work already dispatched is abandoned rather than delivered.

11. The day the child grows up

The transition most products never build, and are therefore wrong about Guardian authority is temporary by nature. A product that grants it at sign-up and never ends it is asserting, silently and indefinitely, that an adult may read a grown person's private work because they were once a child. That is not a small oversight; it is a continuing unlawful basis, and it accrues quietly until the first person notices.

11.1 Two dates, not one — and a year is not enough to schedule either

P0 — Rev 1 collapsed two separate legal facts into one event

Rev 1 deactivated the Guardian edge at the consent threshold. But that edge was defined in §7 as carrying legal guardian authority, and in the UK a 13-year-old may consent to data processing while remaining under guardianship for another five years. Ending the edge at 13 therefore misrecords the legal relationship and, worse, silently removes every platform authorisation that depends on it — a parent would lose the ability to act for their own 14-year-old.

Consent authority and guardianship are separate and expire separately.

DateWhat endsWhat survives
Consent threshold
13/14/16 by jurisdiction		 Guardian consent authority. The young person consents for themselves; existing guardian grants lapse. The Guardian edge stays active. Guardian visibility and account-administration duties continue, because the person is still a minor.
Age of majority
18 in most launch jurisdictions		 The Guardian edge itself. All residual authority. Nothing. They are an adult and the relationship is historical.
P0 — BirthYear cannot schedule either date

§6 chose to store only a birth year, for minimisation. That is right in spirit and makes G5 unimplementable as written: children born in January and December of one year are indistinguishable, so a scheduled job must either revoke authority up to eleven months early — cutting a parent off from a child who is still legally theirs to act for — or keep processing up to eleven months after the young person acquired the right to decide for themselves. Both are wrong, and the second is a continuing unlawful basis.

The resolution keeps minimisation without the ambiguity: the guardian attests the full date of birth at enrolment and the platform computes and stores the two transition dates. Note carefully what this does and does not achieve — see the warning below.

  ConsentAgeOn     computed at enrolment from the attested DOB + jurisdiction

  -- REVEAL it by arithmetic and are protected as if they were it

which stores the derivation, not the DOB.
P0 — these dates are the date of birth, and the spec must not pretend otherwise

A first draft of this section claimed the DOB "is never persisted". That is false in effect. ConsentAgeOn minus the jurisdiction's threshold gives the exact date of birth by arithmetic any reader can do — so these fields are a trivially reversible encoding of the sensitive fact, not a substitute for it.

The claim is withdrawn. The dates are kept because exact scheduling genuinely requires them, and they are classified and protected as birth-date-equivalent personal data — same access controls, same retention, same treatment in a DPIA. Calling a reversible derivation "minimised" would mislead exactly the privacy review this spec exists to inform, which is a worse outcome than storing the date honestly.

What the derivation does buy is narrower: two fields that are each used for one scheduled purpose, rather than a general-purpose birth date available to every query and every export. That is a real data-minimisation gain in use, and it is the only claim now made for it.

P0 — there is no fallback, and the "1 January" one recreated the defect it was meant to soften

A previous draft fell back to 1 January of the birth year when a guardian would not attest an exact date, calling it the conservative end. It is not conservative; it is wrong in a different direction for every boundary at once. A December-born child would enter the looser 6, 10 and 13 safety bands up to eleven months early — the exact defect the exact-date change was made to fix — and MajorityOn could deactivate their guardian's authority before they are legally an adult. It also contradicts two acceptance criteria in this document.

There is no single fallback date that is safe for all four boundaries, because they want opposite conservatism: later is safer for banding, earlier is better for the young person's own rights. So there is no fallback.

  • An exact date of birth is required to enrol a child. Without one, enrolment does not complete.
  • The friction is proportionate: the guardian is already being verified with a payment instrument or a government ID (§8). Attesting a date is a far smaller ask than what they have already done, and the correctness of four scheduled boundaries depends on it.
  • Refusing to invent a date is also the honest position. A guessed boundary is not a smaller error than a missing one — it is the same error, made silently, for every child.

11.2 The consent-threshold transition

At ConsentAgeOn:

  1. Both parties are notified in advance — the child and the guardians — so the transition is expected rather than abrupt.
  2. The young person is offered their own account, and the platform grants the credentials to bind their existing PartyId to a new UserId. They keep everything: the party is the same person throughout, which is exactly why ADR-015 keyed membership on party rather than user.
  3. Guardian consents lapse and the young person consents for themselves from that point — see §11.3, because "consents for themselves" had no valid record shape. The Guardian edge stays active until MajorityOn (§11.1) — they are still a minor, and their guardian retains the duties that come with that.
  4. Workspace ownership does not transfer, because it was already theirs.
    P0 — a previous draft specified two incompatible ownership models at once §12 says this spec's whole contribution is that the child owns their own world, with a guardian able to act for them through the resolver. §11 then said ownership "transfers to them" at age-up — which either contradicts that, or invokes 089 §9.2's transfer path, which also migrates billing claims and can refuse outright when the recipient lacks capacity. A young person could have been locked out of their own childhood work by a quota check on the day they became entitled to it.
    • Age-up is an authority change, not a transfer. OwnerPartyId is unchanged because it already names them; what ends is the guardian's ability to act on their behalf.
    • Billing is the part that does move, and 089 already separates it: OwnerPartyId and the billing subscriber are distinct fields precisely so a parent can pay for a workspace a child owns. At age-up the billing subscriber moves to the young person's own subscription — or the workspace stays billed to the family, which is often what everyone wants and should be offered.
    • A capacity refusal must never orphan them from their work. If neither billing arrangement can be established, the workspace remains theirs and readable; it is sync and quota that pause, not access — the same rule as a lapsed entitlement in Spec 090.
  5. If they do not respond, the account is suspended rather than deleted or continued. Continuing would be processing without a basis; deleting would destroy years of a person's creative work at the moment they became entitled to it. Suspension is the only defensible middle, and the data is retained for a defined window so they can claim it.

The quota consequence is real and worth planning for: a transferred workspace must move its claims to whoever is now billed for it, or be refused — which is precisely the machinery 089 §9.2 specifies. Age-up is the ordinary case that machinery exists for, not an exotic one.

11.3 What a self-consent actually looks like

P0 — the schema had no shape for the grant the age-up flow depends on §10 defines GrantedByPartyId as the guardian, and §16 requires every grant to carry a VerificationMethod drawn from the parental-verification mechanisms of §8. So when a young person consents for themselves at ConsentAgeOn, an implementer has two options and both are wrong: leave the account suspended forever, or manufacture guardian-verification data for a person who has no guardian consenting on their behalf. The transition this spec is proudest of had nowhere to write its result.

  • It is genuinely a verification, not an absence of one: the person proved who they are by signing in to an account bound to their own party. That is the same standard any adult self-consent on this platform meets.
  • Guardian-verification methods remain invalid for a self-grant, so the two cannot be confused in either direction.

12. What guardian authority unlocks

Three things elsewhere are currently blocked, deferred or made awkward by its absence.

Blocked thingTodayWith this spec
Child-owned workspaces Spec 089 makes the parent the owner, because the share resolver asks whether the granting party owns the workspace and a parent acting for a child would fail that check. Recorded as a v1 compromise in the capability brief. The child owns their own world. The resolver accepts a caller who holds an active Guardian edge to the owner, so a parent can share on their behalf without ownership being a fiction.
Acting for a child generally No authority model, so every such case needs a bespoke workaround. One question — "is there an active Guardian edge from caller to subject?" — answerable in one query, usable by any module.
Refusing lawfully Nothing to check, so nothing can be refused. IConsentReader gates the operations that need it — §12.1.

12.1 Enforcement, and where it must live

A consent check the product performs is not a control The lesson from Spec 089 §8.1 applies unchanged: if the endpoint belongs to the platform, the platform must do the checking. A product-side consent check in front of a platform endpoint is decoration, and for an open-source or scriptable client it is not even that. Consent is enforced server-side, at the operation, and fails closed — an absent record refuses, exactly as an unclassified mutating tool throws rather than running. 
Gated on an active, unexpired, unrevoked grant:

  modify a child's profile               -> service-core
  share a child's work outside the family-> sharing-external
  process a child's recorded voice       -> voice
  any use of a child's content for
    training or product improvement      -> improvement

  dispatch generation                    -> RESOLVE THE ROUTE FIRST (§12.3)
    inputs leave the device              -> generation-disclosure  (§9)
    inputs stay on the device            -> service-core only

Absent grant -> refuse. Never "log and continue", never a warning banner.

ENROLMENT is the exception, and is gated differently -- §12.2.

12.2 Enrolment cannot be gated on a consent that does not exist yet

P0 — the bootstrap deadlock in Rev 1 Rev 1 gated "create a child's profile" on a service-core grant. But a ConsentGrant names a SubjectPartyId, and the child does not exist until that gated operation succeeds — so no grant can exist when the check runs, and it fails closed forever. Enrolment was unreachable. The tempting fix is an ungated "create profile" endpoint, which would reopen the very hole the gate exists to close.

Enrolment is instead one atomic operation on IConsentService, gated on the guardian rather than on the child: 

EnrolChildAsync(guardianPartyId, childDetails, purposes, verification)

  PRECONDITION: the GUARDIAN is verified (§8). That is the gate, and it
  is checkable before the child exists.

  IN ONE TRANSACTION:
    create the child Party: attested DOB -> ConsentAgeOn, MajorityOn,
                            and the SAFETY band dates (Spec 096); the
                            DOB column itself is not kept (§6)
    create the Guardian edge          (the ONLY path that may -- §7.2)
    write the service-core ConsentGrant and any purposes granted
    add the child to the family Group (Spec 086)

  All of it, or none of it. There is no window in which a child party
  exists without a guardian, and no endpoint that creates one alone.
  • There is no general child-profile-creation endpoint. Its absence is what keeps the gate meaningful — a caller cannot create a child now and consent later.
  • Subsequent modification is gated on service-core normally, since by then the grant exists.
  • An enrolment whose verification fails creates no party, no relationship, no consent and no group membership — but it is not true that it writes nothing.
    The verification record must survive the rollback that discards everything else A previous draft said a failed enrolment "writes nothing", which contradicts §13's requirement of one AnkConsentVerifications row per attempt including failures — the whole point being that repeated failures are a signal worth seeing. Rolled back with the transaction, that signal is destroyed precisely in the case it exists for. The record also cannot be keyed on the child, who does not exist and never will for a failed attempt.
    • Verification is recorded outside the enrolment transaction, in its own committed write, before the transaction is attempted.
    • It is keyed on the guardian party plus an EnrolmentAttemptId minted at the start of the flow — the correlation key that lets a successful attempt be tied to the child later, and a failed one to be counted without needing a subject at all.
    • So "repeated failed attempts" is a pattern per guardian, which is the right unit anyway: a guardian failing verification against several children is more interesting than one failing repeatedly against one.

12.3 Local generation must not require disclosure consent

P0 — Rev 1 contradicted itself between §10.1 and §12.1 §10.1 says refusing generation-disclosure "leaves a usable product only if local generation exists" — an explicit argument for building it. §12.1 then gated every generation on that purpose, which refuses the local path too. A family declining to send their child's words to third parties would have been denied the privacy-preserving option they were declining in favour of.
  • The route is resolved before the consent check, not after. Local execution requires service-core only.
  • The check is on egress, not on generation. The question consent was actually obtained about is whether this child's words leave the family's device.
  • A route that falls back to remote is a disclosure and needs the purpose. A local job silently retried in the cloud on failure would be exactly the disclosure the family refused — so the fallback is refused rather than taken, and the product says why.
  • This makes local generation a consent feature as well as a privacy and cost one, strengthening the §9 argument for building it.
P0 — safety classification is also egress, and a first draft of this section missed it

"Nothing leaves the device" was too quick. Spec 096 requires every child-facing generation to pass output classification, and its classifiers are external vendors. So a locally generated story is still sent to a third party — to be judged rather than to be written, but sent all the same. A family who refused generation-disclosure would either have their child's content disclosed anyway, or have no safety-compliant local path at all.

And safety is not refusable. 096 fails closed and applies identically on every tier, so "generate locally without classification" is not an option we can offer.

RoutePurposes requiredAvailable when
Remote generation service-core + generation-disclosure Always. The child's input goes to a model vendor and to a classifier vendor.
Local generation, remote classification service-core + safety-classification A narrower, separately-named purpose: the content is judged externally but never authored externally. Materially less disclosure, and honestly described as still being some.
Fully local service-core only Requires on-device classifiers, which do not exist yet. Until they do, this route is unavailable — and the spec says so rather than implying otherwise.

The honest consequence: refusing all disclosure today means no generation, not local generation. The fully-local route is a real goal and a real prerequisite — on-device safety classification — and it is worth stating as an unmet one rather than letting §10.1 imply a capability we do not have. It also sharpens the case for on-device work: it is what makes "your child's words never leave this machine" a claim we could actually make.

13. Persistence

  • AnkConsentGrants — the record in §10. Tenant-scoped, Ank-prefixed, and unique on (TenantId, SubjectPartyId, Purpose) among active rows — deliberately excluding TermsVersion.
    P0 — including the version in the key defeats the purpose it exists for Rev 1 keyed on (…, Purpose, TermsVersion), which permits an active v1 grant and an active v2 grant simultaneously. Since IConsentReader asks only about subject and purpose, it would find the stale v1 and authorise — meaning a material terms change would not actually invalidate anything unless every caller independently knew the current version, which is exactly the coupling a reader contract exists to avoid. Granting a new version therefore atomically revokes the prior one in the same transaction, and the index makes a second active row impossible rather than merely unlikely.

    Supersede-on-grant is still not sufficient by itself — a second defect in the same mechanism. It fires only when a guardian responds. Between publishing a material change and that response the v1 row stays active and IConsentReader keeps authorising, so a guardian who never answers goes on authorising processing under terms this spec calls invalid, indefinitely — which is exactly the state re-consent exists to prevent. Publication of a new current TermsVersion therefore revokes every affected active grant at publication, atomically, without waiting for a replacement. See §10.2.
  • AnkConsentVerifications — one row per verification attempt including failures, since a pattern of failed attempts on one child is itself a signal worth having.
  • Party gains BirthYear, ConsentBand and SafetyBand as separate stored fields (§6), and the computed transition dates ConsentAgeOn and MajorityOn (§11.1). The attested date of birth is used to compute them and is never persisted.
  • PartyRelationshipTypes gains Guardian, which touches the three PartyService validation sites that read Codes.
  • The existing AnkPartyConsents table is not dropped in this spec.
    P0 — it has live consumers, one of which is a subject-access flow

    Rev 1 planned to drop it as dead scaffolding. It is not: CustomerDataService exports these rows in a customer data bundle (:96-101) and imports them (:258), and CustomerAdminService reads them for an admin view (:402-411). Dropping it would destroy imported consent data and break a data-export path — in a specification whose entire purpose is getting consent right.

    Retirement is therefore a sequenced migration, not a DROP:

    1. Add AnkConsentGrants alongside. Nothing is removed.
    2. Archive existing rows into AnkLegacyConsents — a separate table preserving them verbatim.
      Not a backfill into ConsentGrant — the earlier plan to do that was incoherent A previous draft backfilled these as grants with no GrantedByPartyId, while §16 requires that every grant carries one and cannot be written without it. The legacy rows also have no TermsVersion and no Jurisdiction, which the same invariant requires. Implemented literally, either the migration could not insert them or the new schema would have to abandon the invariants that make it worth having. Sentinels would be worse: they put "unknown" inside the field whose whole job is to answer who consented.

      A separate archive keeps both things true. No historical data is lost, the export and import consumers keep their rows, and ConsentGrant's invariants hold unconditionally because nothing violating them is ever written to it.
      • AnkLegacyConsents is append-only through one controlled path, and otherwise read-only — an archive, not a second consent mechanism.
        Strictly read-only left restored bundles with nowhere to go Step 3 keeps pre-migration export bundles importable. Those bundles carry none of GrantedByPartyId, TermsVersion or Jurisdiction, so they cannot enter AnkConsentGrants — and a strictly read-only archive refuses them too. Restoring a customer's own backup would have failed with no valid destination, which is a data-loss bug wearing an invariant's clothes.

        So the archive accepts writes from the legacy-bundle importer alone: append-only, audited, and marked with the bundle version it came from. Nothing else may write to it, it still authorises nothing, and IConsentReader still never reads it — the invariant that mattered is intact, and the one that was merely tidy is relaxed.
      • IConsentReader never consults it, so a legacy row cannot authorise anything — the correct treatment for consent obtained before any verification existed.
      • It answers historical questions and keeps the data-export bundle whole. It grants nothing.
    3. Repoint the three consumers at the new table, keeping the export bundle's shape backward-compatible so existing bundles still import.
    4. Then drop AnkPartyConsents, in a later migration, once nothing reads it — verified by grep without a head truncating the result, which is how Rev 1 got this wrong.
  • All migrations CLI-generated against AonikDbContext per CLAUDE.md.
Retention, which cuts both ways Consent records must be kept long enough to evidence compliance — including after the consent is withdrawn, since the question a regulator asks is whether processing was lawful at the time. But they are themselves records about children. The rule: retain the grant and its outcome for the statutory period; retain no verification artefact beyond the outcome — no document images, no card numbers, no call recordings. VerificationRef points at an outcome, never at evidence we hold.

16. Acceptance criteria

  1. A Guardian edge is never inferred from Mother, Father or Child, and a kinship edge alone grants no authority.
  2. PartyService.CreateRelationshipAsync and its update path refuse Guardian, proven by a test that drives them the way OrderService does — with a caller-supplied code. Every code marked privileged is refused by the same test, so a future one inherits the protection.
  3. A child, their guardian edge and their first consent are created in one transaction or not at all; there is no endpoint that creates a child party alone.
  4. Granting a new TermsVersion revokes the prior version atomically, and two active grants for one subject and purpose cannot coexist — proven on LocalDB, where the filtered unique index is real.
  5. A generation routed locally proceeds without a generation-disclosure grant, but still requires safety-classification while classifiers are external (§12.3) — service-core alone is not sufficient for any route that exists today. A local job is refused rather than silently retried remotely when the local route fails.
  6. Crossing ConsentAgeOn lapses guardian consents while leaving the Guardian edge active; only MajorityOn deactivates the edge.
  7. Both transitions are scheduled from stored dates, and the attested date of birth is not persisted anywhere after they are computed.
  8. The three existing AnkPartyConsents consumers — customer data export, import, and the admin view — continue to work across the migration, and no consent row is destroyed by it.
  9. Archived legacy consents live in AnkLegacyConsents, are never consulted by IConsentReader, and no row lacking GrantedByPartyId, TermsVersion or Jurisdiction is ever written to AnkConsentGrants.
  10. Publishing a new current TermsVersion revokes affected active grants at publication, without waiting for a replacement grant — and processing for those purposes stops until re-consent.
  11. A failed enrolment persists its verification attempt, keyed on the guardian and an EnrolmentAttemptId, while creating no party, relationship, consent or membership.
  12. A generation routed locally still requires safety-classification while classifiers are external; a family refusing it is refused generation, not silently given unclassified output.
  13. Enrolment requires an exact attested date of birth and completes without one under no circumstances; no boundary date is ever inferred from a year.
  14. ConsentBand and SafetyBand are separate stored fields, and neither is derived from the other.
  15. A second guardian can be added to an existing child through AddGuardianAsync — verified to the same standard, authorised by an existing active guardian, and audited — without enrolling a duplicate child. Multiplicity and withdrawal-wins are therefore reachable.
  16. A self-consent has GrantedByPartyId == SubjectPartyId and VerificationMethod = self-authenticated; that method is rejected for any grant where they differ, or where the subject is below ConsentAgeOn.
  17. Age-up does not invoke a workspace ownership transfer — OwnerPartyId already names the young person — and a billing-capacity refusal never removes their access to their own work.
  18. A pre-migration export bundle still restores after the migration, through the controlled legacy-import path, and nothing it writes can authorise anything.
  19. ConsentAgeOn and MajorityOn are handled as birth-date-equivalent personal data — same access controls, retention and DPIA treatment as a stored date of birth would receive.
  20. A child party may have several active guardians, each able to act independently.
  21. Any one guardian's withdrawal takes immediate effect regardless of other active grants, and re-granting is a fresh, deliberate act.
  22. A consent record always names GrantedByPartyId, VerificationMethod, TermsVersion and Jurisdiction. A row missing any of them cannot be written.
  23. Each purpose is granted and revoked independently, and everything except service-core defaults to not granted.
  24. An unmapped jurisdiction resolves to 16 and the strictest method.
  25. A tenant may raise the threshold above the statutory age and cannot lower it below.
  26. A material terms change invalidates existing grants for the affected purposes; the old grant does not silently extend.
  27. Every gated operation refuses server-side without an active grant — proven by calling each endpoint directly, with no product-side check in the path.
  28. Withdrawal abandons in-flight generation rather than delivering it.
  29. A party crossing ConsentAgeOn has guardian consents lapsed, an account offered, and workspace ownership transferred — retaining all their work, and with the Guardian edge still active.
  30. A party crossing MajorityOn has the Guardian edge deactivated. No earlier date deactivates it.
  31. An unclaimed aged-up account is suspended, not deleted and not continued.
  32. No verification artefact is retained — no document image, card number or recording. Only outcomes and references.
  33. Consent records survive withdrawal for the statutory retention period, since lawfulness is judged at the time of processing.
  34. dotnet build and dotnet test Aonik.sln green, plus the LocalDB lane for the filtered unique index on active grants.
