Spec 096 Status: Draft (Rev 5) — ready to implement Size: Large Module: Aonik.Ai

Content safety for child-facing generation

Arke Kids asks a generative model to produce a story or a video for a six-year-old. Sometimes it will produce something else. Neither Aonik nor Arke has any answer to this today — a grep across the solution finds no moderation, no classification, no safety configuration of any kind. This spec is the answer, and like Spec 095 it is a condition of launching Arke Kids rather than a feature of it.

1. Summary

Spec 095 governs whether we may process a child's data. This one governs what we may produce for them. They are independent obligations and neither substitutes for the other: perfect consent does not make a distressing image acceptable, and a clean classifier does not make unlawful processing lawful.

This spec adds:

  • A safety boundary at the generation call — every dispatch on behalf of a child party passes through it, and it fails closed.
  • Layered controls: constrain what can be asked, choose and configure the model, classify what comes back, and keep a record. No layer is trusted alone.
  • Structural constraint as the primary lever, because narrowing what can be generated beats filtering what was.
  • A defined response to a detection, to a false positive, and to the case where a child's own words are the worrying part.

2. Why "mostly safe" is not a target

The failure here is not statistical

Most quality problems are rates: a 0.1% error rate on invoice categorisation is a good system. This is not that. One frightening image reaching one seven-year-old is a complete failure — for that child, for their parent, and for the product, which will be described by what it did that once and not by what it did correctly a million times.

"The model produced it, not us" is not a defence anyone outside engineering accepts, and it should not be. We chose the model, we chose the prompt, we chose to show the result to a child.

Three consequences shape everything below:

  • Fail closed. When a check errors, times out, or is unavailable, the content is not shown. An unavailable classifier means an unavailable feature, not an unchecked one.
  • Defence in depth, no single point of trust. Every layer will miss things. The design assumes that rather than hoping otherwise.
  • Prefer narrowing to filtering. A generation that cannot produce the bad output beats a filter that catches it afterwards — §7.

3. Where safety lives, and the seam it appears to break

An apparent contradiction, worth resolving explicitly rather than quietly

ADR-016 and Spec 089 are categorical: the platform stores a workspace and never opens a file. If Aonik.Workspaces ever learns what a canon entry is, the seam has failed. But content safety must inspect content. Read naively, this spec and that ADR cannot both be right.

They are both right, because they operate at different moments.

a child types an idea │ ▼ ┌──────────────────────┐ │ GENERATION BOUNDARY │ ← safety lives HERE (Aonik.Ai) │ input check │ content is in flight, not yet a file; │ model + settings │ it is a request and a response, and │ output check │ inspecting it is the whole job └──────────────────────┘ │ passed ▼ the product writes a file │ ▼ ┌──────────────────────┐ │ Aonik.Workspaces │ ← NEVER inspects (ADR-016) │ path, hash, size │ by the time bytes arrive here they have │ bytes are opaque │ already been judged └──────────────────────┘

Safety is a property of generation, not of storage. By the time content reaches a workspace it has already passed the boundary, and the storage layer's ignorance is preserved intact. This also happens to be the correct place on the merits: it is the only point where the prompt, the model, the age band and the response are all in hand at once. A storage-layer scanner would have the bytes and none of the context.

A test that keeps this honest If a file arrives in a workspace by any route other than generation — an import, a sync from another device, a file the parent dropped in — this spec does not inspect it, and neither does the platform. That is a real, deliberate limit rather than an oversight: Arke Kids is not a virus scanner or a content filter for the family's own files, and a product that quietly began scanning everything a family stored would be a different and much worse thing. Safety covers what we generate, which is what we are responsible for.

4. Scope (in / out)

4.1 In scope

  • The safety boundary around every generation dispatched on behalf of a child party.
  • Input classification, provider and model selection, output classification, and structural constraint.
  • Age bands, and how they change what is permitted.
  • Behaviour on detection, on classifier failure, and on false positives.
  • Handling of the case where a child's input suggests they may be at risk.
  • Incident records, retention, and what a parent can be told after the fact.

4.2 Out of scope

  • Anything stored rather than generated — §3. Imports, syncs and user files are not scanned.
  • Arke Studio. Its users are adults working on their own machines with their own keys, and it is MIT-licensed and offline. Imposing a moderation layer there would be both unenforceable and a different product.
  • Consent and lawful basis — Spec 095.
  • Building our own classifiers. This spec consumes classification; training models is not in it.
  • Moderating human-to-human communication. Arke Kids has no child-to-stranger messaging, and this spec does not make it safe to add — that would be a materially different risk surface needing its own work.

5. What actually goes wrong

A spec that will not name the failures is useless to whoever implements the checks. These are the categories, stated plainly and without embellishment.

CategoryHow it arrivesNote
Sexual content Rarely from an innocent prompt; more often from ambiguous wording, or a deliberate attempt. Zero tolerance at every band. No context makes this acceptable output for a child.
Graphic violence and gore Very often from an innocent prompt. "The knight fights the dragon" is a normal request from a six-year-old and has a wide output distribution. The most common real failure. Needs banding rather than a blanket rule — see §9.
Frightening or distressing imagery Body horror, threatening figures, darkness — often from prompts containing no forbidden word at all. Hardest to classify, because "frightening" is age-relative and not a standard classifier label. Structural constraint carries more weight here than filtering.
Self-harm and suicide content Sometimes from a child's own words rather than the model's. Blocked as output and treated as a signal about the child — §11.
Hate and demeaning depiction Stereotyped depictions of a group, often without any slur present. A child's story about "a person from another country" should not return a caricature.
Real-person likeness A child naming a classmate, a teacher, or a celebrity. Generating imagery of a named real child is unacceptable regardless of intent, and this is easy to overlook because the prompt looks harmless.
Confident falsehood The model states something untrue as fact to an audience with no capacity to evaluate it. Not "unsafe" in the usual sense, and worth stating: a credulous audience makes ordinary hallucination a harm of its own.
Prompt injection through content Text inside an imported file or an image instructing the model. Treat all content as data, never as instruction — the same rule that governs every other agent surface in this platform.

L3 is necessary and nowhere near sufficient

Provider safety settings are built for general audiences. "Safe for a general audience" and "safe for a six-year-old" are different thresholds, and the gap between them contains most of what actually goes wrong here — the frightening, the violent-but-not-graphic, the age-inappropriately intense.

They also change without notice, differ between vendors, and are outside our control. Turn every provider safety setting to its strictest available level, then design as though it were not there.

7. The strongest lever is structural

The design instinct worth internalising Narrowing what can be generated beats filtering what was. A filter is an adversarial contest against a model's entire output distribution, run in milliseconds, on every request, forever. A constrained request space is a design decision made once. The second is more reliable and vastly cheaper, and it is underused because filtering feels like the more direct answer.
ConstraintEffect
Curated characters The child picks from a designed, pre-vetted cast rather than describing a person freely. Removes the entire real-person-likeness category, and most of the frightening-figure category, at a stroke.
Story templates Structure supplied, the child supplies the variables. A "a journey to find the lost X" frame with a chosen X has a far narrower output distribution than open text.
Bounded scene vocabulary Settings, moods and actions from a designed list. The younger the band, the smaller the list.
Reference-bound imagery Generation anchored to approved reference images — a technique Arke Studio already has for character consistency, doing safety duty here.
Pre-generated libraries For the youngest band, some content need not be generated live at all. Reviewed once, served many times, and unsafe output is impossible by construction.

This is a product decision as much as an engineering one, and it points somewhere useful: free-text prompting by young children is the single riskiest feature in Arke Kids. It is worth asking whether the youngest band needs it, rather than assuming it and then defending it with classifiers.

8. Who is the human in the loop

Arke's core mechanic does not transfer, and the reason matters

Arke Studio's premise is that "nothing enters the authored record without a human accept." It is the product's best idea and this platform reuses it twice — in sync divergence and in the tiered mutation gate. The obvious move is to reuse it a third time.

It does not work here. In Studio the human reviewing the proposal is the author protecting their own record. In Kids the recipient is the person being protected — so putting the child in the review seat means showing them the thing they were supposed to be shielded from, and asking them to judge it. The accept gate cannot be the safety control when the accepting human is the one at risk.

So the human layer is the guardian, and it is deliberately optional and secondary:

  • Guardian pre-review — generated content is held until an adult approves it. Default on for the youngest band, available to any parent who wants it.
  • Guardian visibility — an adult can always see what has been generated for their child, whether or not they pre-reviewed it. This is a Children's Code parental-controls expectation and simply the right default.
  • Never the primary control. A parent is not a moderation queue and will approve in bulk within a week. Automated layers must be able to stand alone; guardian review is reassurance, not the mechanism.
  • Transparency to the child. Where an adult is reviewing or can see their work, the child is told, in age-appropriate terms. The Children's Code is explicit that covert parental monitoring is not acceptable, and a product that lets a parent watch a child secretly is one we should not build regardless.

9. Age bands

Bands change three things: what may be asked, how strict the classifiers are, and whether an adult is in the path.

P0 — a birth year cannot schedule a band change either A previous draft derived the band from Spec 095's BirthYear. That is the same defect 095 already had to fix for its own transitions: a year cannot say when a child turns 6, 10 or 13. Advance bands on 1 January and a December-born five-year-old gets a ten-year-old's prompts, thresholds and guardian defaults almost twelve months early; advance them at year end and a January-born child sits in the wrong band for nearly a year. The first error is the dangerous one, and it is the one a naïve implementation makes.

095 already collects an exact date transiently at enrolment, so the band boundaries are computed and persisted there alongside ConsentAgeOn and MajorityOn — and, like those, they are birth-date-equivalent data and protected as such.
BandRequest spaceThresholdsGuardian review
Under 6 Curated only. No free text. Strictest. Mild peril blocked. On by default.
6–9 Templates with bounded free text. Strict. Cartoon peril permitted; injury, weapons-as-threat, and darkness are not. Optional, default on.
10–12 Free text with input classification. Adventure-appropriate. Real jeopardy allowed; graphic depiction is not. Optional, default off. Visibility remains.
13 to majority Free text. Teen-appropriate. Off. Visibility remains while the Guardian edge does — to 18, not to 13.
P0 — the last band runs to majority, not to the consent threshold

A previous draft ended the table at "13+ under the threshold". Once Spec 095 §11.1 separated the consent threshold from the end of guardianship, that row stopped covering anyone: a UK 13-year-old begins self-consenting and remains a minor with an active guardian edge until 18. So ages 13 to 17 matched no row at all — five years of minors with undefined classifier thresholds and undefined guardian visibility, which under a fail-closed reading means the strictest band and under a permissive one means none.

Safety banding tracks minority, not consent capacity. A young person acquiring the right to decide about their own data does not thereby stop being someone this spec protects — the two questions are unrelated, and conflating them was a consequence of fixing the other spec.

Unknown age is not the loosest band A party with no attested birth year is treated as under 6 — the strictest — not as an adult. Same reasoning as 095's unmapped jurisdiction: the wrong-way default is the one that ends badly, and the cost of being over-strict is a support conversation rather than an incident.

10. What happens on a detection

Three distinct cases, and conflating them produces a product that is either unsafe or unusable.

10.1 Output blocked

  • The child sees a plain, unalarming message — that this one did not work and they can try again — and never the content, never a category name, never a red warning. A seven-year-old told they triggered a "violence filter" learns they did something wrong; they did not.
  • An incident record is written (§13), and the guardian can see it, but is not alarmed by a notification for an ordinary block.
  • The generation is not billed. Charging a family a story credit for content we refused to show is indefensible — the meter reservation is released (Spec 087), which is exactly what its reserve/release lifecycle is for.

10.2 The check itself fails

  • Fail closed. Classifier down, timed out, or erroring means the content is not delivered.
  • The child sees the same plain message; a failure to check is not their problem to understand.
  • Operationally this is an outage and must page. A silently degraded classifier that returns "safe" on error is the worst possible defect here, and is the specific thing the fail-closed rule exists to prevent.
  • Not billed, same as above.

10.3 False positives, which will outnumber true ones

The failure mode of a safety system nobody plans for A knight fighting a dragon is the single most common request a six-year-old makes. A classifier strict enough to block genuinely violent output will block a great deal of ordinary children's storytelling — fairy tales are full of wolves, witches, and characters in real danger, and always have been. A product that refuses half of them is broken in a way that is less catastrophic than the alternative but far more common, and it teaches children that the product does not work.
  • Measure both error rates — and be honest that they are measured differently. A safety system reporting only what it caught is reporting half its behaviour. But the two halves are not symmetric:
    • False positives are countable, but not from releases alone. A guardian release is a decision to allow, not evidence the classifier erred (§8) — it flags a candidate for review. The rate is assigned by independent review of those candidates, which is available from the first release but is a review output rather than a click count.
    • False negatives are not. By definition they passed and were delivered, so no incident exists and there is no denominator. Rev 1 made per-band false-negative rates a first-release acceptance criterion, which is not implementable — you cannot count what you did not detect, and guardian reports alone catch only the harm someone recognised and bothered to report.
    What replaces the unimplementable criterion A scheduled evaluation rather than a live metric: a held-out corpus of adversarial and ordinary prompts per band, run against the live configuration on a fixed cadence, labelled by named reviewers — a defined owner, not "the team". Plus privacy-safe sampling of delivered output for review, at a rate and under access controls agreed in S0, since reviewing children's content is itself processing that needs a basis and a limit. The corpus gives a denominator; sampling catches what the corpus did not anticipate. Neither is a live rate, and pretending otherwise was the error.
  • Guardian appeal — but not an unconditional release.
    P0 — Rev 1 handed guardians a bypass of the entire safety boundary

    Rev 1 said an adult "can view blocked content and release it to their child", full stop. That is defensible for a blocked dragon fight and indefensible for everything else: the classifier is often right, and the sentence as written let a guardian deliver sexual content, graphic material or explicit self-harm content to a child by clicking through — with L4 described elsewhere in this spec as the last line before the child.

    It is also wrong about who a guardian is. A guardian account is not proof of good intent, and the one category where that matters most is precisely the one where an unconditional release capability is most dangerous.

    CategoryAppeal
    Non-overridable — sexual content, explicit self-harm instruction, and the §12 category No release, by anyone, ever. The content is not viewable by the guardian either. An appeal here is a signal, not a request: it is recorded, and repeated appeals against this category on one account escalate to a person.
    Reviewable — violence, peril, frightening imagery, everything age-banded The guardian may view and release to their own child only. This is where false positives actually live, and where a parent's judgement should outrank a threshold.
    P0 — a release is a decision, not ground truth, and treating it as a label is a dangerous loop

    A previous draft called every successful appeal "exactly the labelled data needed to tune thresholds". It is not. §10.3 says plainly that a parent's judgement may outrank an accurate classification for reviewable content — so a release records that this guardian chose to allow it, which is a different fact from the classifier was wrong.

    Counting them as false positives produces invalid rates, and feeding them back into tuning is worse than useless: it would teach the system to pass content that reviewers still consider unsafe, one permissive parent at a time, with the drift showing up as an improving metric. A safety system that learns from the people it is meant to constrain will converge on constraining nobody.

    • A release is recorded as a guardian decision, and is not a label.
    • A false positive is assigned only by independent safety review — the named reviewers of §10.3's evaluation — looking at the content and the band, not at what a parent chose.
    • Releases are still useful as a signal: a category with many releases is a candidate for review, which is how they should influence thresholds — by directing attention, never by moving one automatically.
    • Nothing in the non-overridable branch is ever released, so nothing there is a false positive we act on. If the classifier is wrong about that category, the fix is the classifier.
  • Tune per band rather than globally, since a threshold correct for a five-year-old is absurd for a twelve-year-old.
  • Retry with constraint before refusing outright: a re-generation with tightened structural constraints (§7) often produces an acceptable version of the same idea, which is a far better outcome for the child than a refusal.

11. When a child's own input is the concern

This will happen, and both easy answers are wrong

Children disclose things in stories. Some of what a child types into a storytelling product will suggest they are frightened, hurt, or being harmed. The two instinctive responses are both wrong: ignoring it because it is out of scope, and building automated detection and escalation because it feels responsible.

The second is worse than it looks. An automated system that flags children as at-risk from story text will be wrong very often — children write about scary things because children write about scary things — and the consequences of being wrong land on a real family. We are a storytelling product, not a safeguarding service, and we must not act like one.

The position, deliberately narrow:

  • We do not run risk-detection classifiers over children's creative writing. Not because it is hard, but because the false-positive consequences are severe and we are unqualified to act on the true positives.
  • Explicit self-harm and suicide content is blocked as output — a model must not generate it for a child — and that is a content rule, not a judgement about the child.
  • Age-appropriate signposting. Where a child's input clearly and explicitly concerns self-harm, the product surfaces a gentle, non-clinical pointer to help, written with professional input. It does not diagnose, alarm, or accuse.
  • Guardian visibility is the honest mechanism. The adults who can actually help already see what their child creates (§8). That is the appropriate channel and it needs no surveillance layer.
  • A defined route for staff. Where a member of staff encounters something concerning through ordinary support work, there is a written path to a named responsible person — not an ad-hoc decision by whoever saw it. This is a policy artefact, drafted with qualified input, and it is not automated.

The one absolute exception is §12, which is not a judgement call and not ours to moderate.

12. Reporting obligations

Non-negotiable, and stated plainly because a spec that omits it is negligent

If child sexual abuse material is ever generated, uploaded or detected on this platform, there are legal obligations to preserve and report it to the relevant authority, and they are not discretionary, not deletable-and-forget, and not something engineering decides in the moment.

The correct engineering behaviour is to make the path exist before it is needed. A written procedure, a named responsible person, a preservation mechanism that does not depend on someone remembering not to delete, and legal counsel engaged in advance. Discovering this obligation during an incident is the worst possible time to learn it.

  • A written procedure exists before launch, drafted with counsel and naming the responsible person and the reporting body for each launch jurisdiction.
  • Preservation is automatic on detection at this category, and overrides ordinary retention limits and deletion requests — including a subject-access deletion, which cannot be used to destroy evidence.
  • Access to preserved material is restricted to the named individuals and every access is logged.
  • Provider terms are checked for their own reporting duties, since generation happens through third parties who have obligations of their own and may act independently.
  • Nothing here is delegated to a classifier's judgement alone: detection at this category escalates to a person, immediately.

13. Evidence, and the tension in keeping it

Two obligations pulling in opposite directions A parent asking "what did your product show my child?" deserves a real answer, which requires keeping records. The Children's Code requires data minimisation for children, and every retained generation is a stored record of a child's imagination. Resolving this by keeping everything forever is the easy answer and the wrong one.
RecordRetentionWhy
Safety decisions — one per attempted delivery: verdict, band, policy version, run ids. No content. Bounded, and short by default. Proposed: 90 days in full, then anonymised — subject party dropped, the aggregate kept for evaluation. A row per generation is a lot of rows about one child, and a previous draft gave it no lifetime at all while using it to pin AiRuns — so the design retained an unbounded history of every child's every generation and held their model runs open indefinitely. That is the opposite of the minimisation this same section argues for. ISafetyRetentionSweeper owns the expiry and the anonymisation, alongside artefacts; the §12 hold overrides both.
Safety incidents — a block, its category, its band, its verdict Longer. Months. Answers the parent's question, tunes the thresholds, evidences that the system works.
Blocked content itself Short. Days, only long enough for guardian appeal. Storing the very thing we judged unsafe for a child, indefinitely, would be perverse. Keep the verdict, discard the artefact.
Delivered content It is the child's work. It lives in their workspace and is theirs. Not a safety record. Governed by 089 and the family's own choices.
Prompts Minimal, and tied to the incident rather than kept as a stream. A log of everything a child ever typed is precisely the dataset the Children's Code exists to discourage.
§12 category Preserved, overriding all of the above. Legal obligation. Not a retention policy decision.

14. Safety costs money

Worth stating because it interacts with the commercial model rather than sitting outside it. Every generation now carries additional classification calls — potentially several, across text and image — so it is slower and more expensive than the raw generation alone.

  • Safety is never a paid tier. The £0 "Peek" tier gets the same protection as the £39.99 one. Selling safety to children would be indefensible, and a cheaper tier that is less safe is not a product decision anyone should be able to make.
  • The cost is in the unit economics of a generation, so it belongs in the pricing of story and video credits rather than in a separate meter.
  • Cheap layers run first. Input classification before dispatch avoids paying for a generation that was never going to be delivered — which is both cheaper and faster.
  • A refused generation releases its meter reservation (§10.1), so families are never billed for what they were not shown.

15. Persistence

  • AnkSafetyDecisions — one row per attempted delivery, not only per block: subject party, safety band, verdict, the categories evaluated, the applied SafetyPolicyVersion, the classifier AiRun ids, and a nullable generation AiRun id.
    Two corrections to this schema

    The generation run must be nullable. An L2 input block happens before dispatch — that is the layer's whole purpose, and it is the cheap fail-closed path — so no generation run exists. A non-null column would force an implementation either to skip persisting the block or to fabricate an AI execution that never happened, corrupting the audit log in the name of completing it. The classifier runs are still required; the generation run is present only for post-dispatch outcomes.

    The applied policy version must be recorded. Versioning AnkSafetyPolicies does not by itself make an old verdict attributable — a reviewer looking at last month's block would have to infer which threshold applied from timestamps, which is guesswork the moment a policy is changed and re-changed. Persisting the version on the decision makes "why was this blocked?" answerable exactly, which is the question §10.3's evaluation is built on.

    P0 — linking runs only through an incident loses the case that matters most A previous draft attached the run ids to AnkSafetyIncidents, and an incident is written only when something is blocked. So a delivered output had a generation AiRun and classifier AiRuns scattered in the audit log with nothing tying them to the verdict that let it through. If that delivery were later identified as a false negative — by a parent, or by the §10.3 evaluation — we could not reconstruct which classifiers judged it safe or on what basis. The audit trail existed for everything except the failures it is for.

    This also carries no more content than before: the decision row stores the verdict and the run references, never the content itself. It is a few hundred bytes per generation, and it is the difference between being able to answer a parent and not.
  • AnkSafetyIncidents — the subset of decisions that blocked, carrying layer, category, the appeal state and the artefact reference. Subject party, age band, verdict, decision, timestamp, and the AiRun ids of the generation and of every classifier call that judged it. Tenant-scoped, Ank-prefixed.
    P0 — a model name is not an audit trail, and rule 4 is not optional

    Rev 1 recorded only a model and provider label, and the classifier contract said nothing about how vendors are called. Implemented literally, a classifier could be integrated directly — creating no AiRun at all — and a block or a delivery could not be traced back to the exact executions that produced it. When a parent asks why their child saw something, "a model called x said it was fine" is not an answer.

    It also breaks a platform rule outright. AONIK's fourth non-negotiable is that every AI action is auditable, recorded as an AiRun — and a safety classifier is an AI action taken on behalf of a child, which is the last place to make an exception.

    • Every AI-backed layer runs through the audited pipeline. Input classification, output classification and the generation itself each produce an AiRun; a classifier that bypasses it is a defect, not an optimisation.
    • The incident stores the run ids, so a verdict is reconstructible: what was asked, which model answered, what each classifier returned, and what was decided.
    • Retention is aligned — an AiRun referenced by a live incident is not swept before the incident is, or the trail breaks exactly when it is needed.
  • AnkSafetyArtefacts — short-lived pointers to blocked content held for appeal, with an expiry and an override for §12.
    P0 — Rev 1 referred to "the sweeper" and never built one An expiry column deletes nothing. Rev 1 named a sweeper in passing, put it in no phase, and gave it to no contract — so as specified, blocked content about children would have been retained indefinitely, contradicting this spec's own short-retention promise and acceptance criterion 11. A retention policy nothing enforces is a statement of intent, and the gap is invisible precisely because the column looks like the mechanism.
    • ISafetyRetentionSweeper as a Quartz job in Aonik.Worker, shipped in S1 with the incident record rather than left to a later phase — the artefacts start accumulating the moment blocking works.
    • Legal-hold exclusion is explicit: an artefact under a §12 preservation flag is skipped and its skip is logged, so preservation and deletion cannot silently contend.
    • Operational tests, not just unit ones: the job runs per tenant (EnforceTenantOnWrites rejects cross-tenant writes, so it commits per tenant like every other scheduled job in this platform), and a monitored failure to run is treated as a retention incident rather than a quiet backlog.
  • AnkSafetyPolicies — thresholds per band and category, as data, so tuning does not require a deployment. Versioned, since a threshold change must be attributable when reviewing an old incident.
  • Migrations CLI-generated against AonikDbContext per CLAUDE.md.

Two things a caller must not be able to say

The safety band is not a request field. A request that carried its own band could claim adult for a six-year-old and skip every threshold and every guardian hold with one value, so SafetyRequest does not have the property at all and the gate reads it through ISafetyBandReader from the record Spec 095's age-transition job maintains. Unresolvable resolves to the strictest band, never to adult.

A permit names the artefact it covers. Carrying only the subject would make it a bearer token: hold any valid permit and you could pair it with a different reference — or pair an image permit with audio — and launder unclassified content through a delivery type that looks checked. The permit carries the modality and the exact content reference, and delivery types verify it.

P0 — a second routing mechanism would breach a platform boundary

A previous draft selected classifier implementations "by modality and vendor, by configuration". That builds a parallel provider-routing mechanism alongside the one the platform already mandates: "no hard-coded model usage; all calls resolve through AiRoutePolicy". Requiring the audited pipeline is not the same rule and does not imply it — the pipeline records whichever vendor was chosen, while routing decides which. Implemented literally, safety would have been the one subsystem making its own model choices, and Spec 060's cost governance and fallback would not have applied to it.

The fallback point is the practical one: §2 fails closed, so a classifier vendor outage takes the product down. Central routing is exactly where a second provider is configured — so routing through it is not merely compliance, it is how F1's redundancy requirement is actually delivered.

The gate must be unbypassable, on the same pattern that already works Spec 032 solved this exact problem for mutating tools: IToolApprovalGate wraps every classified tool, and an unclassified mutating tool throws at build time rather than running ungated. Content safety takes the same shape — a child-facing generation path that has not been routed through IContentSafetyGate fails to construct. A gate a caller can forget to call is not a gate, and this platform has already learned that once.

16.1 Routing is bounded by what the family consented to

P0 — the redundancy argued for in the last revision can silently breach consent

Routing classifiers through AiRoutePolicy was the right correction, and it introduced a new hole. Spec 095 §9 requires the consent text to name the external companies a child's content is sent to, and makes that list part of the versioned terms. But IConsentReader checks only subject and purpose — so a routing edit, or an automatic failover, can send a child's content to a company the family has never heard of, without publishing terms or re-obtaining consent. Nothing in either spec would have caught it.

It is the most likely way this design breaches consent in production, precisely because failover is supposed to be automatic and invisible.

  • Route selection for a child-facing generation is intersected with the provider list in the subject's active TermsVersion. A provider outside it is not a candidate, at any priority.
  • Adding a provider is a terms change, with the publication-time revocation that implies — not a configuration edit.
  • Failover outside the list refuses and fails closed, rather than switching. The child sees the ordinary message; the operator sees an alert.
  • So name every candidate provider up front, including failover ones.
    The operational consequence, which is genuinely awkward and worth accepting A vendor added during an incident cannot be used until terms are published and families re-consent — which is exactly when you least want that constraint. The answer is to consent to the full candidate set in advance rather than to the current one, so failover stays within it. That makes the terms slightly longer and slightly less specific, and it is the right trade: a family who agreed to a named set of companies is being told the truth, whereas one who agreed to a single provider and got a different one is not.

17.1 S6 outcome — video does not ship, and two locks say so

Implementation note — 2026-08-13 · not a product decision

F6 is product's to resolve and has not been. Rather than leave that as an absence, the S6 work shipped the disabled state itself, because "nobody registered a video classifier" and "video is off" look identical in a codebase right up until someone registers one.

  • Video is off by configuration — SafetyOptions.EnabledModalities lists text, image and speech. A request for a disabled modality is refused with a distinct outcome, ModalityDisabled, which records a decision but does not page. A feature nobody turned on cannot be down, and conflating the two teaches an operator to ignore the alert that matters.
  • Enabling it is not sufficient. A classifier for a temporal modality must declare TemporalCoverage.Complete, and one declaring Sampled — or declaring nothing — is refused before it is called. This makes §18.23 executable: the test uses a sampling stub modelling a video whose single harmful frame sits between sample points, so it truthfully returns clean scores and is refused anyway. The refusal cannot depend on the classifier noticing.
  • Speech carries the same requirement, because it has the same hole. Listing only video would have hardened the modality we are not shipping and left the one we are.

So resolving F6 toward complete coverage becomes a configuration change plus one adapter, and resolving it toward sampling fails at the gate rather than in a review. Video staying off remains the spec's sanctioned outcome, and nothing here changes that.

17.2 What S0 no longer gates, and what still has to exist

Decision — 2026-08-13, Michael An earlier revision made S0 a blocking phase requiring counsel and qualified input before any code. That gate is removed; S0 now runs alongside S1. The distinction that matters is between advice, which was gating and no longer is, and obligations and duties of care, which are not advice and do not stop being true because a review was skipped.
ItemWasNow
Band and category thresholds Agreed before code parallel They are §15 policy data, and S1's schema does not depend on their values. Ship the mechanism, tune the numbers.
The §12 reporting procedure Drafted with counsel before code still required, not a code gate This is a legal obligation, not legal advice. The technical parts — preservation that overrides deletion, restricted access, access logging — are built in S1 regardless. What is carried is the written procedure and the named person, and it must exist before the product is put in front of a child, which is a later date than before the first commit.
The §11 staff route Written with qualified input before code still required, not a code gate No code implements it — §11's whole position is that this is not automated. It is a written path to a named person, needed before launch rather than before implementation.
The one thing that does not move F7 — the named responsible person for §11 and §12 — still has to be a person, before a child uses this. Removing the review gate changes when the paperwork is done; it does not remove the duty, and "someone will look at it" remains not a procedure. This is the item most likely to be deferred until it cannot be, which is why it is repeated here rather than left in the open-decisions table.

18. Acceptance criteria

  1. A child-facing generation path not routed through IContentSafetyGate fails to construct, on the Spec 032 pattern.
  2. A classifier that errors or times out causes the content not to be delivered, and pages. It never returns "safe" on failure.
  3. A party with no attested birth year is treated as the strictest band.
  4. Under-6 cannot submit free text.
  5. Provider safety settings are at their strictest available value, and the system still blocks output they permitted — proven with a case that passes the provider and fails L4.
  6. A blocked generation releases its meter reservation; no family is billed for content they were not shown.
  7. A blocked generation shows the child a plain, unalarming message with no category name and no warning styling.
  8. An incident record exists for every block, and a guardian can see their own child's records.
  9. A guardian can view and release blocked content in the reviewable branch only, and the release is recorded as a guardian decision — never as a false-positive label, and never fed directly into threshold tuning.
  10. False-positive rates are assigned per band by independent review, not derived from guardian releases. False-negatives are reported from the scheduled evaluation of §10.3 — a named-owner corpus plus privacy-safe sampling — and not claimed as a live metric.
  11. A guardian cannot view or release content blocked in a non-overridable category, and repeated appeals against that category escalate to a person.
  12. Every safety verdict records the AiRun ids of the generation and of each classifier call, and no classifier reaches a vendor outside the audited pipeline.
  13. An AiRun referenced by a live decision or incident is not swept before it is.
  14. Every attempted delivery writes a decision record — not only blocks — carrying the verdict, the applied policy version and the run ids, so a delivery later identified as a false negative can be reconstructed exactly rather than inferred from timestamps.
  15. An L2 input block persists a decision and an incident with a null generation run, and no fabricated AiRun is ever written for a generation that did not happen.
  16. Safety decisions are expired and anonymised on a bounded schedule by ISafetyRetentionSweeper, and an AiRun is unpinned when its decision is — so the pin cannot retain runs indefinitely.
  17. No classifier resolves its provider or model outside AiRoutePolicy — and routing for a child-facing generation is restricted to providers named in that subject's active terms (§16.1). A policy edit cannot redirect a child's content to an unnamed company.
  18. Failover to a provider not named in the active terms is a refusal, not a switch. The generation fails closed and the child sees the ordinary "did not work" message.
  19. A person aged 13 to majority resolves to a defined band with defined thresholds — no age below majority falls outside the table.
  20. Band transitions fire on stored exact dates, so a December-born child does not enter an older band in January.
  21. The retention sweeper runs, deletes expired artefacts, and skips anything under a §12 hold — with the skip logged. A sweeper that fails to run is surfaced as a retention incident, not a silent backlog.
  22. Generated speech is classified before a child hears it, with its own fail-closed behaviour; voice does not inherit coverage from any other modality.
  23. If video ships, every delivered frame has been covered — demonstrated with a video containing one harmful frame between sample points. Frame sampling alone never satisfies this criterion.
  24. Blocked content itself is deleted on its short retention; the incident record survives.
  25. Detection at the §12 category preserves material, overrides deletion including a subject-access request, restricts access to named individuals, logs every access, and escalates to a person immediately.
  26. Where guardian review or visibility is active, the child is told in age-appropriate terms.
  27. Nothing in this spec inspects a file that arrived by import or sync — the ADR-016 seam is intact and Aonik.Workspaces gains no content type.
  28. Safety behaviour is identical on the £0 tier and every paid tier.
  29. dotnet build and dotnet test Aonik.sln green.

#QuestionWhoLeaning
F1 Which classification vendors, and are they contractually suitable for children's data? Engineering + counsel At least two, for redundancy — a single vendor outage otherwise takes the whole product down, since we fail closed. Their data-handling terms are a 095 §9 disclosure question too.
F2 Does the youngest band get free-text prompting at all? Product No. It is the riskiest feature in the product and the least necessary for that age, who are better served by a beautiful curated experience than an open box.
F3 Is guardian pre-review on by default beyond the youngest band? Product Implemented as leaning. Default on to age 9, then off with visibility retained. Parents who want more can opt in — and a guardian may switch it off even for the youngest band, because §8 is explicit that pre-review is reassurance rather than the control. Guardian preferences stop applying at majority, so a stale setting cannot outlive the authority to change it.
F4 How long is blocked content retained for appeal? Product + counsel 7 days. Long enough for a parent to notice; short enough that we are not warehousing rejected material.
F5 Do we publish our safety approach? Product Yes, in plain language. Parents choosing a product for their child deserve to know how it works, and the Children's Code expects transparency. It is also a genuine differentiator against products that say nothing.
F6 Video classification is expensive and slow. Sample frames, or classify whole? Product, not engineering Neither — and this is now a product decision about whether to ship video at all. Rev 1 leaned to frame sampling with density as a policy value. That is an engineering convenience that contradicts this spec's own premise: sampling cannot establish that a video is safe, because a harmful frame or a short sequence between sample points is delivered even when every sampled frame passes. It cannot coexist with L4 being the last line before a child, nor with zero tolerance on §5's first category.

The honest options are complete temporal coverage, short generations that can be covered affordably, pre-generated and human-reviewed video for the youngest bands, or video stays off. Sampling is not among them, and "we sampled every second frame" is not a sentence anyone wants to say to a parent.
F7 Who is the named responsible person for §11 and §12? Michael Must be a person, before launch. "Someone will look at it" is not a procedure, and this is the decision most likely to be deferred until it cannot be.
